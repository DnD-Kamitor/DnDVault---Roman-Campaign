Ch4 — The God's Demand

SESSION 6  ·  L6  ·  5 players (not Brian)  ·  Village → North Grove → South Grove → Dungeon Entrance
Five soldiers with no officer carry a drowned boy and a humming box into a village that has quietly emptied. The village seer wants the box carried to the sacrifice stones in the South Grove, and the boy's bones lie under the old altar in the North Grove. The session ends when the bone serpents at the dungeon's real entrance fall and the stair down lies open.

Arrival (read aloud)
The village smells of woodsmoke and old bread. Two fires burn where ten should be. Half the doors stand open on empty rooms. A woman at a quern stops grinding and watches you without moving. Then her eyes find the boy on your back, and her hands go still on the stone. At the far end of the street, a tall old man in a wolf-pelt is sharpening a flint knife on a whetstone, slowly, and singing to it.

Dramatic Question: Will the village trust them enough to tell the truth, before the seer walks south with their box?

The god of the grove: Grannus (and how to speak to him)

Who: Grannus, the old Celtic sun god of healing springs, worshipped here by the Helvetii before the Suebi came. Romans call him Apollo Grannus; his consort is Sirona, the spring-goddess. His greatest shrines are the hot springs of this very forest (Aquae, today's Baden-Baden). Soldiers from Vindolanda know him by another name: their fort's Belatucadrus is the same sun under the war-mask (Religion DC 13 makes the link). Rome painted Mars over him; the chieftains cut Tiwaz over his marks. He is starved, not dead.

On the map (SacredTreePublic): the golden tree is his. The two pools are Sirona's. The blue lights in the stones are his eyes, half-shut.

Who: a Suebian clan, settled here by Rome   Where: deep in the Abnoba (the Black Forest), north of the upper Rhine, near today's Swiss border. Inside the province of Germania Superior: the frontier wall lies far to the north-east, but Roman roads skirt the forest and nobody official comes in here. Lake Brigantium (Constance) to the south-east; Roman Helvetia across the Rhine.   When: 175 AD.
Who they are, in three lines
  • Planted on old land. A generation ago Rome moved this clan off the war-torn Danube and settled them on empty forest inside the province, in return for recruits and peace. Before them this was Helvetian (Celtic) country; the Helvetii moved south across the Rhine long before Caesar, and geographers still call these hills the Helvetian Desert. The old sun-marks on the grove stones are theirs.
  • Rome's subjects, not Rome's people. Not citizens (peregrini, "foreigners" on Roman land). They owe sons to the auxiliaries and a yearly tax in cattle and hides. They trade with the roads, resent the levy, and tell Varus stories by the fire anyway.
  • Thinned, not conquered. The two chieftains took the young men south to the stones. Few came back. The village has been quietly emptying for a year.
What the party sees
HousesLong timber halls with thatch to the ground: people at one end, cattle at the other, one fire between. Small sunken huts for weaving. A palisade of stakes, with a gap nobody has mended.
MenThe Suebian knot: hair combed back and tied in a knot over the right temple. It marks a free man; slaves may not wear it. Trousers, a short cloak pinned with a bronze brooch, bare chests in summer.
WomenLong sleeveless wool dresses pinned at both shoulders, a linen shift underneath, hair braided or covered once married. Keys and a knife on the belt mean she runs a household.
WealthCounted in cattle, not coin. Roman things are prized and shown off: a red Samian bowl on a shelf, a bronze jug, a few denarii worn as jewellery.

How they live together
The kinEverything runs through family. Wrong one man and you have wronged his kin. A killing is settled with blood-price (cattle) or with a feud.
The thingFree men and women meet at the new or full moon to decide matters. Agreement is shown by clashing spears on shields; disagreement by a low murmur. With the chieftains gone, the thing has not met in months.

The grove custom (Tacitus wrote it down): the Suebi gather at a sacred grove where blood is offered, and no one enters the grove unless bound with a cord, to show they stand below the god. A PC who ties a cord round their wrist before entering the North Grove: advantage on the first wisp interaction. The chieftains twisted this old rite into murder.
The dead

  • A body left unburned and unsung cannot find the way. That is why the boy walks: nobody burned him, nobody spoke his name.
  • Traitors and cowards are pressed into the bog under a hurdle, so they can never come back. (The Farbog's drowned men were the chieftains' "tests".)

175 AD: what everyone is talking about
  • The war on the Danube. Eight years of the Marcomannic War. The emperor has just made peace with the Marcomanni and Quadi this year; nobody believes it will hold.
  • The plague. The Antonine plague still comes in waves. Half-empty villages are not unusual, which is how the chieftains hid what they were doing.
  • Resettlement. Rome is moving beaten Germans inside the empire to farm empty land. This clan is one of the older cases; newer ones arrive every year.
  • Rumours from the East. Word on the roads says a general in Syria (Avidius Cassius) has called himself emperor. Romans are nervous; the levy officers ask for more sons.
Greeks in this world (for Greek PCs or villagers)
  • Greek letters on old stones. Caesar found the Helvetii's census lists written in Greek script; Celtic druids kept records in Greek letters. Tacitus (c. 98 AD) says tombs with Greek inscriptions still stand on the border of Germania and Raetia, right here. A Greek reader can read worn letters under the chieftains' TIWAZ marks on the South stones: ΗΛΙΟΣ, "sun". Evidence for Thusnelda (counts like the bones).
  • Greek is the empire's second language. Army doctors, engineers and clerks are very often Greek. The emperor himself writes his private notes in Greek, in his Danube camp, this very decade.
  • The Rhône road. Greek Massalia (Roman Massilia, still Greek-speaking) has sent wine and goods up the Rhône into these hills for 700 years. Sigrun's best stock comes that way; she haggles in Greek happily.
  • To the villagers a Greek is "a Roman who reads": respected for learning, trusted a little more than a Latin soldier. A Greek PC gets advantage on one village skill-challenge roll for reading the old stones aloud.
